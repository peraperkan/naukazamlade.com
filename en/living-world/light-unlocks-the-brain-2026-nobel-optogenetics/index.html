Light unlocks the brain: the Nobel Prize for optogenetics and the first steps toward restoring sight

The 2026 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine went to Peter Hegemann, Georg Nagel and Karl Deisseroth for discoveries that made possible: controlling the activity of selected cells with light. The story begins with a tiny green alga, passes through laboratories investigating brain circuits and reaches the first, still experimental attempts to restore a measure of visual perception in people with severely damaged retinas. This is a remarkable achievement, but it is neither a cure for blindness nor a way to read or program human thoughts.

12 min readLevel: IntermediatePublished: 13 Oct 2026

Why is observing the brain not enough?

When we observe brain activity during learning or fear, we can find cells whose signals change. But two events occurring together do not automatically tell us which is cause and which is effect. A group of neurons might initiate behavior, relay a signal from another part of the brain or merely react to something that has already happened. To test causation, scientists must deliberately change the cells’ activity and then observe what changes in behavior or bodily function.

Electrodes can act very quickly, but their current may also affect nerve fibers and several kinds of nearby cells. Drugs can sometimes target certain receptors more selectively, yet their effects often last far longer than a neuron’s electrical pulse. brought a different combination: a genetic procedure enables a chosen cell population to respond to light, and researchers control the timing of illumination precisely. Its selectivity depends on how the gene is delivered, which cells express it and where the light reaches; a light pulse alone does not mean that only one neuron was targeted.

Decades earlier, Francis Crick had considered how useful it would be to switch particular kinds of brain cells on or off rapidly. In a 1979 essay he emphasized the need for better control over specific neuron types, and later he suggested light as a possible tool. What was missing was a molecule able to turn a light signal into an electrical response in a cell.

What did a green alga bring to neuroscience?

The single-celled alga Chlamydomonas reinhardtii swims toward or away from a light source. For an algal cell, this helps it find conditions for photosynthesis. For scientists, it posed a question: what molecule detects light and turns that detection into a signal for movement? The answer began with studies of light-sensitive proteins in the alga’s cell membrane.

Peter Hegemann spent years studying how Chlamydomonas responds to light. His research linked the alga’s behavior to membrane channels that open after absorbing a light signal. The connection between light and ion flow was crucial: it showed that a single protein could directly alter a cell’s electrical state.

Georg Nagel and colleagues helped characterize those proteins. A 2002 paper described one light-activated channel, and a 2003 paper another, now known as . Research on an alga had gained an application that was far from obvious at the outset: its protein could serve as a tool to control other cells.

Blue light changes the shape of , opening a path for positively charged ions across the membrane. Their movement changes the cell’s electrical voltage and can trigger a nerve impulse. The speed of that response mattered greatly because neurons exchange signals on very short timescales.

Researchers do not place an entire alga inside a brain. They use genetic instructions to make one protein; the target cell produces that protein and inserts it into its membrane. Retinal, the molecule that receives light, is already present in mammalian tissues in amounts sufficient for many early experiments. Later work developed other light-sensitive proteins, including ones that can suppress neuronal activity. Different molecular switches respond to different colors and cause different electrical effects.

How does light become an experiment?

First researchers select a group of cells and a way to make those cells produce the right . A viral carrier or an inherited genetic line can deliver the gene; a promoter and other genetic elements help determine where the protein is made. Next, light is brought to those cells, often through an optical fiber, while the scientists measure electrical activity or the animal’s behavior. Every stage influences how precisely the result can be interpreted.

In 2005 Karl Deisseroth and collaborators, including Ed Boyden, Feng Zhang, Ernst Bamberg and Georg Nagel, showed that could rapidly trigger impulses in cultured mammalian neurons. That study used a to introduce the gene. Deisseroth’s contribution helped turn a molecular discovery into a method for probing nerve-cell function. Use in the brain of a living mouse followed in 2007. Scientists could then compare the exact timing of illumination with recorded neural impulses and the animal’s actions. What have we learned about memory and behavior?

let researchers test whether a particular neural circuit could contribute to a behavior. In a well-known 2012 experiment, Susumu Tonegawa’s team marked cells that had been active while a mouse learned to associate one environment with an unpleasant experience. Later, light reactivated some of those cells in a different environment, and the mouse showed a response associated with recalling fear. Deisseroth was a co-author. The study supports a role for the marked circuit in retrieving a learned response, but it does not show that a whole memory lives in a single group of neurons or that every memory can simply be switched on at will.

Similar experiments in animal models have helped probe networks involved in movement, reward, mood and the effects of neurological disease. A result in a mouse does not automatically become a treatment for Parkinson’s disease or depression in humans. Moving from experiment to therapy requires evidence of benefit, safety and reproducibility in appropriate clinical studies.

When photoreceptors disappear, what remains in the eye?

The retina is more than a layer of cells that receives light. Rods and cones are : they convert light stimulation into a neural signal. Other retinal cells process that signal, and ganglion cells send it onward through the optic nerve. The arrangement of these layers explains why optogenetic treatment targets cells other than the original .

That raised the possibility of giving those remaining cells a direct response to light. The disease also alters the rest of the retina, however; surviving cells are not perfectly preserved in every eye. How was partial visual perception first restored?

In a study published in 2021, a man with very advanced received an viral vector carrying a gene for the ChrimsonR in one eye. Here is a vehicle that brings genetic instructions to target cells; this should not be imagined as an entire virus permanently inserting itself into a person’s DNA. Such carriers are used because genes cannot simply pass through target-cell membranes unaided.

The patient wore special goggles. A camera recorded the surroundings, and the device converted the information into light pulses suited to activating the modified retinal cells. Time was needed for the protein to be produced and for the person to learn how to interpret the new signal. With the goggles, he could locate, touch or count some objects in controlled tasks more often than without them.

This was important evidence for partially functional visual perception. It was not a return to normal sight: the result concerned one patient, required special equipment and was obtained in limited conditions. Researchers associated with the teams of José-Alain Sahel and Botond Roska developed and tested the approach; it would be inaccurate to attribute this clinical result to Deisseroth’s laboratory.

A newer, small clinical study published in October 2026 included ten people with advanced . Seven showed increased light sensitivity; six met a threshold the authors judged clinically meaningful, although that criterion had not been fully specified in advance. Researchers also recorded adverse events: 34 eye-related events in nine participants, including one severe, temporary retinal artery occlusion that resolved. The findings support continued testing, not a claim that the method is risk-free or ready for routine use.

The people behind the discovery and the limits of its promise

Many more people than the three Nobel laureates worked along the path from alga to clinical trial: biophysicists who studied the proteins, specialists in gene-delivery systems, neuroscientists who tested behavior and physicians who investigated treatment. The Nobel Prize singles out a few pivotal contributions, but the history remains a team effort. According to Stanford’s account of the day he learned of the award, Deisseroth received the call in the middle of the night in California. Soon afterward he returned to the family routine and made six sandwiches for the three children still living at home.

is a powerful way to test hypotheses about how cells work. Therapeutic use deep in the human brain still faces major obstacles: safe gene delivery, getting light into deep tissue, duration of the effect and unintended changes in activity. Animal experiments show what certain neural circuits can do; they do not justify portraying human memories, feelings or decisions as switches that we can simply operate today. The strongest human result in this story so far concerns experimental, partial restoration of perception in some forms of retinal blindness.

That is the real strength of the discovery: understanding how an alga responds to light produced a tool for asking more precise questions about the brain, and researchers are now carefully testing whether it can help patients. Future clinical results will show how far that path truly leads.
