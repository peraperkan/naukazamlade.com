The first images of black holes: how we photographed what does not shine

The orange ring around the dark center has become one of the most famous scientific images of our time. However, it is not a snapshot of the "surface" of a black hole, nor a shot from a huge telescope. It is a carefully reconstructed image of the radio radiation of gas whose light is bent by an extremely strong gravitational field.

Why do we call the Moon the Moon? A story of the sky, language and measuring time

Jupiter has Europa, Io, Ganymede and Callisto. Saturn has Titan and Encelad. And Earth has — the Moon. At first glance, our nearest celestial neighbour seems to have missed out on a special name while all the others received more interesting ones. Yet the Moon itself provided the starting point for naming other bodies of this kind. Its name connects the history of astronomy with the history of language: long before telescopes and space probes, changes in its appearance helped people count days and follow the passage of time.

A wobbling asteroid: what Lucy learned about Donaldjohanson

In images from NASA's Lucy spacecraft, asteroid Donaldjohanson looks like an elongated peanut: two uneven lobes joined by a narrow neck. Its shape is only the beginning of the story. An analysis published in June 2026 shows that the roughly eight-kilometre body does not simply turn around one steady axis; it slowly tumbles. Its surface also preserves chemical evidence of contact with liquid water, probably from a time before the present asteroid formed. How can a spacecraft discover all this during a brief flyby?
