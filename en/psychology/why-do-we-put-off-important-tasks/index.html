Why do we put off what matters? The psychology of procrastination

Delay often begins with a very persuasive thought: just this one more thing, then I will start. The problem is not every postponed obligation but a delay we choose despite expecting it to make things harder later. Research shows that unpleasant feelings, how we evaluate a task and the conditions in which we work all matter to that decision.

6 min readLevel: IntermediatePublished: 27 Sept 2026

When delay becomes procrastination

Postponing a project because equipment is missing or another job is more important can be rational. usually means voluntarily delaying an intended activity despite expecting the delay to worsen its outcome. The boundary therefore does not depend solely on the calendar. What matters is what we want, what we can realistically do and how we assess the consequences.

Imagine a student who knows a paper needs two sources and an outline of the argument. Instead of reading the first source, he tidies his desk, checks email and chooses a heading font. These are all actions, but none addresses the main problem. It is not necessary for him to lack the desire to succeed: he may strongly want a good paper while avoiding the step that frightens him. This example explains why advice to simply want success more often misses the actual obstacle.

Brief relief can have a delayed cost

In 2013 Fuschia Sirois and Timothy Pychyl described as a problem in which short-term mood repair can take priority over a long-term goal. A task may cause boredom, uncertainty or fear of evaluation. Moving to a more pleasant activity then quickly removes some discomfort. That relief can increase the likelihood of repeating the same avoidance.

does not mean that emotions are enemies of reason. They may indicate that a task is unclear, too large or connected with a real risk. The problem arises when moving away becomes the only response. A useful step is distinguishing not knowing the first move, fearing a mistake and not having enough time. Each obstacle calls for a different response. The single label of laziness obscures both the reasons and possible solutions.

How we evaluate an unfamiliar task

Javier Granados Samayoa and Russell Fazio examined the relationship between delay and . When we have no stable attitude towards something, we may rely more on unpleasant or pleasant features of earlier similar experiences. Their 2024 paper included studies linking these evaluations to delayed activity. One part also examined students’ deadlines for participating in research.

The experimental part used a computer game in which feedback taught participants about favourable and unfavourable outcomes. The researchers tried to shift their weighting of these outcomes and then track delay. The results offer an interesting indication that an initial impression of a task can matter. They nevertheless do not prove that one game solves school or that every delay comes from the same mechanism. Scientific American presented the topic in an article by the researchers themselves, and our treatment preserves the distinction between a particular experiment and general practical advice.

Why the environment belongs in the explanation

Fuschia Sirois’s 2023 review emphasises that stressful conditions can increase vulnerability to . When a task is unpleasant and our capacity to cope with discomfort is reduced, an easily available distraction becomes more attractive. This does not mean everyone under stress procrastinates or that stress explains all . It shows why behaviour should be considered alongside the conditions in which it occurs.

Studying in a quiet room differs from trying to study between family obligations, interruptions and unclear expectations from a teacher. The difference can persist even with equal motivation. Before changing a schedule, it is worth checking whether the goal is sufficiently specific. Studying biology defines neither a beginning nor an endpoint. Explaining three differences between mitosis and meiosis without looking at the book provides a testable first task and a measure of progress.

From an intention to a decision with a time and place

An links a recognisable situation with a specific action. Instead of deciding to work hard tonight, we can write: after dinner, I will open the sources at my desk and write three sentences of an outline. In their 2006 , Peter Gollwitzer and Paschal Sheeran combined research on such plans and goal attainment. On average these plans helped people act on intentions, although most tasks examined were not exclusively about .

This principle should therefore not be presented as a guaranteed cure for everyone. A good plan must be feasible: scheduling two hours of focused work when other obligations are known to intervene makes little sense. Including an expected obstacle can help: if I do not understand the question, I will first note exactly what is unclear. Uncertainty then becomes the next action rather than a reason to shut the job down. A small beginning is not the same as lowering the standards for the finished work.

What intervention research says

A 2018 and examined psychological methods aimed at reducing . Results suggested possible improvement, but also few high-quality studies and differences between approaches. This is a good reason not to promise transformation through one universal technique. Combining studies does not automatically remove their weaknesses, and results depend on what was measured and how long participants were followed.

In an everyday attempt, track the beginning of the actual work rather than merely hours spent at a desk. Note when you opened relevant material, what you completed and where an obstacle arose. If one step is repeatedly avoided, perhaps it needs to be broken down or explained more precisely. If delay persistently and seriously disrupts school and everyday life, discussing it with a school psychologist can help clarify the reasons. That means assessing a particular situation, not proving that every occasional setback is a disorder.

An attempt for your next obligation

Choose one task you are delaying and write down what makes it unpleasant. Then identify the smallest action that really changes the state of the project: reading a paragraph, solving the first item or writing a question to a teacher. Prepare what it needs and link it to a moment you can recognise. Afterwards, check whether you actually moved towards the goal rather than repackaging the same avoidance.

This demonstration does not prove which psychological theory your case supports. Its value is making the problem more visible and specific. If the first plan fails, you can change the step size, timing or availability of distractions. Treating an error as information about working conditions makes the next attempt easier to improve. is therefore more usefully understood as a pattern of decisions we can investigate than as a permanent verdict on someone’s character.
