Why do we change our minds in a group? Conformity and peer influence

When everyone in a group gives the same answer, confidence in our own judgement can be hard to preserve. Sometimes the majority teaches us something new; sometimes it merely leads us to say publicly what we do not believe. Research on shows that the situation matters more than a simple division into independent and easily led people.

5 min readLevel: IntermediatePublished: 27 Sept 2026

Two reasons for following others

means adapting a judgement or behaviour to the actual or imagined influence of others. occurs when we treat others’ answers as evidence: perhaps they know something I do not. concerns acceptance in a group: I do not want to be the only person who differs. The same person in the same situation can experience both influences, but they are different.

Imagine a student who calculates 12 independently while everyone at the table says 18. He might change his answer because someone else’s calculation reveals his mistake. He could also remain doubtful but say 18 to avoid prolonging the discussion. Observing the public answer alone does not reveal whether his belief actually changed. A researcher must therefore consider what is being measured: speech before a group, private judgement or behaviour in a later situation.

Asch’s lines: a simple task and a complex decision

In the 1950s Solomon Asch used comparisons of line lengths. Participants had to match one line with an equally long one among several choices. Most people in the group were the experimenter’s associates, deliberately giving the same wrong answer on selected trials. The actual participant responded after hearing several such answers. Without this pressure the task was very easy.

In the classic version, participants followed the incorrect majority on roughly one-third of critical trials. About one-quarter never did so. These numbers do not mean a third of humanity is always easily led: the first describes responses on particular trials, while the second describes participants across the whole sequence. This was also not an experiment about obedience to authority like Milgram’s, but about group influence on a public judgement. The distinction prevents a familiar experiment from becoming a story about something it did not investigate.

What one different voice changes

Asch varied the conditions, including the majority’s unanimity. When an ally appeared who did not repeat the wrong answer, the pressure decreased. This shows that group influence depends on a united presentation as well as the number of people. One different voice can reveal room for independent judgement. However, specific effect sizes depend on the experimental version and should not be transferred to every classroom or online discussion.

For group work, we can turn this into a simple procedure: everyone first writes a judgement independently and discussion begins afterwards. The first voice then does not immediately frame every subsequent answer. A second step is asking for reasons rather than merely counting votes. Four students copying the same calculation do not provide four independent checks. Agreement carries more information when people reached a conclusion by different routes.

Why a classic experiment is not a universal law

The original samples were narrow, mostly male students in a particular social setting. Lines on cards are not equivalent to a political position, choice of friends or moral decision. A 2024 of research examines how findings vary with characteristics of tasks, groups and context. Such research does not justify one fixed rate for every culture and age group.

A limited sample does not make an experiment worthless. It can clearly show pressure under well-controlled conditions. The next step is checking whether the finding recurs after changes to age, participant roles, response format or the importance of the decision. This distinguishes research from an anecdote. A story about one friend can illustrate a problem but cannot measure how often it occurs.

Peer influence can help

Peer influence is not synonymous with pressure to do something harmful. A 2021 review by Brett Laursen and Rene Veenstra describes how similarity between peers arises both through choosing friends and through influencing one another. Shared habits can support belonging, cooperation and learning. It is therefore wrong to label every common interest a loss of independence.

A 2020 study by Kathy Do and colleagues involved 39 adolescents aged 12 to 14. When changing attitudes, they more often followed positive influences and peer attitudes towards . The authors also examined brain responses when participants encountered opposing views. Judgements were measured, so the results do not prove that actual behaviour changed too. In a school setting, a group that values asking questions can encourage precisely the behaviour another group discourages.

Choosing friends or changing because of friends?

At least two accounts can explain why two friends have similar study habits. Perhaps they became close because their habits were similar from the start. Perhaps one adopted the other’s approach during the friendship. A third possibility is that both respond to the same teacher or conditions. A single measurement of their similarity cannot distinguish these explanations.

In 2014 Science News presented research that also followed young people abstaining from unhealthy drinking in friendship groups. The topic showed that a majority does not always function as a simple command everyone follows. Several successive measurements help investigate influence, but even they do not remove every group difference. When reading a headline about peer pressure, check whether change over time was measured or merely similarity at one moment.

Preserving cooperation and your own judgement

Independence does not mean automatically rejecting the majority. Changing your view when someone offers good evidence can show careful thinking. A useful question is what in the explanation changed your judgement. If the only answer is that everyone said so, pause and check the problem again. If the answer is a clear fact previously unknown to you, accepting another view can be justified.

In a group task, we can separate collecting suggestions from evaluating them. Everyone first offers a solution, then all suggestions are checked against the same criteria. A minority view gets a chance to be explained, without automatically becoming true merely because it is a minority view. A good group lets people present reasons, report doubts and jointly correct poor judgements rather than requiring everyone to think alike. This form of cooperation uses social influence to examine ideas.
