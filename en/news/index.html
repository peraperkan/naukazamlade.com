A wobbling asteroid: what Lucy learned about Donaldjohanson

In images from NASA's Lucy spacecraft, asteroid Donaldjohanson looks like an elongated peanut: two uneven lobes joined by a narrow neck. Its shape is only the beginning of the story. An analysis published in June 2026 shows that the roughly eight-kilometre body does not simply turn around one steady axis; it slowly tumbles. Its surface also preserves chemical evidence of contact with liquid water, probably from a time before the present asteroid formed. How can a spacecraft discover all this during a brief flyby?

The unseen residents of tree bark: how microbes change the path of methane

When we look at a tree, we usually think of leaves taking up carbon dioxide through photosynthesis. Researchers have looked at the bark as well. It houses communities of microorganisms that use gases moving through the trunk or arriving from the air. An Australian team reported in 2026 that, when oxygen is available, these microbes can consume methane, hydrogen and carbon monoxide. A tree is therefore also a habitat whose inhabitants affect the exchange of gases between soil and atmosphere.

How does the brain change as we learn? What neuroplasticity really means

When we first try to play a melody, solve a physics problem or speak a sentence in a new language, the task is slow and demands attention. Practice makes it easier. That change is more than a feeling: experience affects how nerve cells communicate and how whole networks take part in a task. We call this capacity neuroplasticity. It remains with us throughout life, although its form and extent depend on age, brain region and the skill involved.

Can a sound we cannot hear affect our mood?

A faint vibration runs through an old building's ventilation system. We cannot clearly hear it, yet the space feels uncomfortable. Is that only expectation, or can very low frequencies affect experience? A small laboratory study published in 2026 found links between infrasound exposure, irritability, ratings of music and cortisol levels. The finding is intriguing, but it does not prove that “haunted houses” have an acoustic explanation.
